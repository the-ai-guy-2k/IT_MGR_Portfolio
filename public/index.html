Biography

From the Desktop, Behind the Endpoint, Back to the Team

My path to IT leadership did not begin with a management title. It began next to the user — supporting the desktop, the workstation, and the mission in front of me.

I entered the U.S. Navy in 2002 and spent roughly fourteen years in mission-critical technology work. The lesson was larger than the equipment: systems had owners, people had responsibilities, failures had consequences, and the mission still had to move. Leadership, accountability, planning, continuity, and technical judgment developed alongside engineering skill.

In civilian engineering, I carried that discipline into healthcare, defense research, distributed enterprise infrastructure, security, cloud, and automation. Those roles exposed me to the realities support and operations leaders inherit every day — operational risk, technical debt, dependencies, change, outages, specialist knowledge, and competing priorities.

TAIG IT Solutions changed the level of responsibility. As Founder and Manager, I became accountable for more than the technical answer: deciding what mattered, understanding client intent, shaping services, coordinating people and providers, managing relationships, setting priorities, and owning outcomes.

Engineering remains an advantage, not the destination. It gives me the technical credibility to understand what sits behind an endpoint problem. Management experience gives me the foundation to lead the people, service, and operations around it.

The proposition is straightforward: put an experienced manager with deep engineering judgment in responsibility for desktop support and IT operations, and evaluate me by whether the team becomes clearer, more reliable, better aligned, and better able to serve the user.

Leadership philosophy

Advance the Established Truth

  1. Establish Intent

    Know what the organization is trying to accomplish and what success actually means.

  2. Establish Current Truth

    Understand the real state: people, systems, resources, risks, constraints, dependencies, evidence, and unresolved work.

  3. Advance the Truth

    Make decisions, assign work, execute, validate, update the picture, and continue until the intended outcome becomes reality.

Plans can change when reality changes. Responsibility does not. My management approach keeps decisions tied to evidence and activity tied to mission.

People Leadership

Set clear ownership. Delegate at the right level. Give specialists room to exercise their discipline. Make priorities visible. Surface risk early. Develop people. Hold the team — and management — accountable for outcomes, not theater.

Decision Standard

  • What are we trying to accomplish?
  • What is true now?
  • What is blocking us?
  • Who owns the next action?
  • What evidence will tell us it worked?

Desktop Support / EUC leadership

30 / 60 / 90-Day Plan

This is a method for entering a support organization, not a prewritten diagnosis of one. Technology follows the operational need. I don't arrive assuming automation is the answer.

  1. Days 1–30

    Understand

    Learn the people, users, environment, service expectations, ticket patterns, tools, escalation paths, documentation, current metrics, and recurring pain points. Establish Current Truth before prescribing major change.

  2. Days 31–60

    Stabilize & Improve

    Identify recurring friction, documentation gaps, workflow problems, training needs, escalation patterns, service bottlenecks, and low-risk improvement opportunities. Stabilize what is materially hurting the user or the team.

  3. Days 61–90

    Advance

    Where evidence supports it: improve processes and documentation, strengthen team visibility, automate appropriate repetitive work, prototype useful internal tools, improve operational reporting, and establish measurable improvement cycles.
